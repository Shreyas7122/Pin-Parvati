From Chandigarh, I eventually reached Bhuntar.

From there, I took an HRTC bus towards Chojh village.

This was the point where I felt like I was actually entering the mighty Parvati Valley.

The road became narrower and narrower.

At some stretches, the bus seemed to be travelling just inches away from falling straight down the mountain.

I moved to the front seat beside the driver.

It was probably the best seat in the bus.

The driver offered me some snacks and started talking to me. I wanted to have them, but there was one small problem.

My strict dal-chawal-heavy preparation diet had given me a heat ulcer.

So I had to politely refuse.

Instead, they suggested I eat some coconut because apparently that would help with the ulcer.

It was a small thing.

But it was also becoming a pattern.

Since leaving home, I had been meeting complete strangers, and somehow, they kept looking out for me.

Eventually, I reached Chojh.

And I immediately liked it.

In some ways, it felt more vibrant than Kasol.

There was cool art painted on the walls, plenty of good food options, and somehow, despite being close to such a popular region, it felt much less commercialised.

From the village, I had about a **15-minute hike** to the cafe where Bindu and Jessi lived.

They were the owners.

I reached there, had some amazing food and met Mafia, their dog.

Mafia seemed to know the village better than I did.

At one point, he started walking towards the grocery shop, and I followed him.

A group of kids stopped to play with him. They kept calling Mafia over and talking to him as if he was one of their friends.

Apparently, they were even inviting him to their birthday.

I had only just arrived, and somehow the dog had already introduced me to half the village.

I also got talking to the manager.

He had moved here from Bangalore and had settled into life in the valley.

I took a bath, relaxed for a while and eventually came down to the cafe, where Bindu and Jessi were sitting.

There were locals and other people around, and naturally, the conversation turned towards Pin Parvati.

This was when the scale of what I was about to do started sinking in again.

There was the distance.

There was the altitude.

And Bindu was nervous too.

She hadn't trekked anywhere this far or this high before.

So this wasn't a conversation between someone experienced and someone inexperienced.

We were both about to step into something neither of us had done before.

But Bindu was also genuinely excited.

She had come to the Parvati Valley around six years earlier with Jessi, from somewhere completely different, and the two of them had eventually settled here.

Now she was going to travel through the Parvati Valley towards Spiti.

As far as she knew, nobody from the village had done that before.

For her, there was also something more personal behind the journey.

She wanted to connect with **Parvati Mata**.

Then the conversation shifted towards *jadi booti*.

September was around the time when the flower season was ending and the season for collecting *jadi booti* was beginning.

Some of these plants could be medicinal.

Some could also be harmful.

It was another reminder that the mountains weren't just beautiful landscapes. There was an entire world of knowledge around them that I knew very little about.

The landowner's father was a **separd** there, and through the conversations, I started hearing more about the people and lives connected to the valley.

Some of the people who had settled there while working remotely had explored places nearby, but hadn't gone much further.

And then there were Bindu and Jessi, who had come here six years ago and made this valley their home.

The conversation kept moving.

Pin Parvati.

The valley.

The plants.

Life there.

Stories.

Random jokes.

Everything in between.

It was one of those conversations where you don't realise how much time has passed.

And looking back, that entire day had a strange theme to it.

I kept meeting strangers.

And somehow, those strangers kept caring about me.

A conductor stopped his bus to help someone.

People on another bus offered me food and advice.

A dog led me through a village.

And now I was sitting with people who had made this valley their home, talking about the journey I was about to begin.

Eventually, it was time for me to sleep.

I had travelled a long way to get here.

Tomorrow, I would finally start walking.
