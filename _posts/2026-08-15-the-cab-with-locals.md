## The Road to the Mountains

Eventually, it was time to leave Chandigarh.

I got into the shared cab.

There was an uncle and aunty who lived in Kullu, a girl who worked as a social media manager, and another girl who worked in the IT industry in Chandigarh.

The driver started with the usual questions.

*"Aap log kya karte ho? Kahan se ho?"*

And somehow, that simple conversation turned into hours of talking.

The uncle and aunty told us about the floods they had experienced the previous year.

From there, the conversation kept moving.

The floods in Nepal.

Vande Bharat — we even saw the new red one.

Temples.

Challans.

A new tunnel that was almost ready.

Waterfalls.

Maharashtra's culture.

Himachal's culture.

Lahaul-Spiti.

Every topic somehow led to another.

I had barely slept the previous night, but I had completely forgotten about it.

Then they asked me what I was doing in Himachal.

So I told them.

I was going to attempt Pin Parvati.

I explained that I hadn't done a trek anywhere near that long before.

At one point, the conversation turned to driving.

I told them,

**"You can drive for a day, maybe two. Three at the most. Eventually your body gets tired. And even if you love driving, you'll get bored."**

The driver agreed.

It was funny because I was talking about driving, but I realised the same thing could probably be said about almost anything.

You can love something and still get tired doing it.

And that made me think about Pin Parvati.

I hadn't done anything remotely close to it before.

But nobody in that car made me feel like that meant I couldn't.

They listened.

They asked questions.

They stayed surprisingly calm about the whole thing.

And they still had high hopes that I would actually make it.

The conversation kept moving between sarcasm, curiosity and more serious stories.

At some point, it became emotional enough that the driver started singing.

He had an incredible voice.

What made it even funnier was that the people who travelled with him regularly apparently had no idea he could sing like that.

A few hours earlier, all of us had been complete strangers.

Now we were sitting in a car in the middle of Himachal, talking about our lives, arguing about random things, laughing and listening to our driver sing.

I like travelling for moments like this.

You meet people for a few hours, and somehow they tell you things they might not even tell someone they know.

---

And then the landscape started changing.

The green became impossible to ignore.

Waterfalls appeared between the mountains.

We passed dams — some with still, almost perfectly blue water, and others where the water was flowing brown and fast.

I had seen Himachal before.

But never like this.

I had mostly experienced it in its harsher, more barren form.

This was different.

Everything was green.

The mountains felt alive.

There was also a new tunnel that was supposed to be completed around the 15th. It was expected to cut almost two hours from the journey.

I remember looking at it and thinking that I was probably going to miss travelling through this engineering marvel by just a few days.

But honestly, I didn't mind.

For once, I wasn't in a hurry to reach anywhere.

I just wanted to keep looking out of the window.

I had finally entered the mountains.

And I had never seen Himachal this green before.
