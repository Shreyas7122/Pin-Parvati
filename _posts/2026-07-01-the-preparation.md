While preparing, I realized something slightly ridiculous.

**I had never done a Himalayan trek before.**

I'd done treks in Maharashtra. I'd ridden through Spiti. I'd travelled quite a bit.

But Pin Parvati was different.

Ten days. High altitude. Carrying my own load. Glaciers. Rivers. Camps.

The mental preparation, strangely enough, wasn't the difficult part.

I had been thinking about this trek for almost a year. By the time it was actually happening, I had already convinced myself that I was going to do it.

The biggest thing I had gathered from making that decision was **belief**.

I started asking my friends about the treks they had done. Some of them had been to 4,000-metre peaks and told me how difficult and tiring they were.

And I remember thinking:

*I'm going to do a 5,000-metre pass.*

Maybe that was overconfidence. Maybe it was just belief.

The strange thing about belief is that once you have it, your brain starts looking for evidence to support it.

The problem was, at that point, I didn't have much evidence.

I had never done anything close to Pin Parvati.

But I had already made up my mind that I was going to find out.

So instead of trying to convince myself that I was capable of it, I started preparing like I was.

I continued my regular gym training, but cardio became a much bigger part of it. I started treating endurance as seriously as strength.

Then came the stairs.

I'd put around 15 kg into my backpack and climb up and down 16 floors—sometimes three to five times in a day.

It wasn't glamorous training.

It was repetitive, exhausting and probably looked slightly ridiculous to anyone watching.

But every time I finished, I had a little more evidence that I could handle the load.

I also started preparing for something I hadn't really thought about before: **what I was going to eat for ten days.**

I kept asking Bhaiji what food would actually be available during the expedition because I didn't want to reach the mountains and suddenly discover that my body hated the food.

So I started eating the kind of food we'd have on the trek—dal-chawal, rajma, chole, daliya.

It wasn't exactly the diet I would have chosen for myself every day.

It definitely made me feel the heat sometimes.

But I stuck to it and kept working toward my nutrition goals.

Then there were the shoes.

That decision was actually easier than I expected. I had guidance from my Dad, a rock climber and a few other people whose opinions I trusted.

Eventually, the shoes arrived.

And suddenly all that preparation became physical.

These weren't just hiking shoes I'd bought because they looked good.

I was going to use them for Pin Parvati.

So I took them on my first proper test.

**14 kilometres.**

With around **13 kg on my back.**

It was the first time I could look at what I had been preparing for and think:

*Okay. Maybe I really can do this.*
